# Namibia: data protection, e-commerce, VAT and public procurement as at November 2026

Research date: 25 November 2026. Scope: Namibia only. Each claim has a source. Where sources conflict, the conflict is stated. "Unconfirmed" means I found no primary or reliable dated source.

## 1. Has the Data Protection Bill been passed and commenced? Status, dates, regulator and obligations

### Takeaway
No. As at November 2026 Namibia has no data protection statute in force, no data protection regulator and no statutory duties on registration, breach notification or cross-border transfer. The Data Protection Bill is still pending. The latest dated primary-press evidence (New Era, 27 January 2026) says it was due to go back to the Cabinet Committee on Legislation, with no tabling date. I found no reliable evidence that it was tabled, passed, assented to or gazetted at any point in 2026.

### Cited Findings
**Status timeline (latest first)**
- 27 January 2026: ICT Minister Emma Theofelus said drafting and consultation on the Data Protection Bill were complete. The bill was "ready for resubmission" to the Cabinet Committee on Legislation for validation, after which Parliament would process it. No tabling date was given. The Cybercrime Bill was still in draft, with stakeholder consultations starting 2 February 2026. — [New Era, "Data legislations under final polishing", 27 Jan 2026](https://neweralive.na/data-legislations-under-final-polishing/)
- 20 March 2026: DLA Piper's handbook, last modified on that date, says Namibia has no comprehensive data privacy statute. It also lists no national data protection authority, no registration requirement, no restrictions on collection or processing, no transfer restrictions, no data security requirement, no breach notification duty and no enforcement mechanism. It describes the Draft Data Protection Bill, 2021 as published but not enacted. — [DLA Piper Data Protection Laws of the World: Namibia](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=NA)
- 3 October 2025: After a data breach at the Namibia Students Financial Assistance Fund (NSFAF), in which students' personal information was published on the Fund's website, the head of Nam-CSIRT said the data protection law "needs to be finalised urgently". A CRAN spokesperson said institutions lack clear breach-handling and recourse protocols because there is no comprehensive legal framework. The report says no data protection act has been enacted and that the planned September 2025 tabling had not been confirmed. — [The Namibian, 3 Oct 2025](https://www.namibian.com.na/calls-for-urgent-finalisation-of-data-protection-law-after-nsfaf-data-breach/)
- 18 August 2025: The bill had been in development for about 12 years, and the minister expected tabling in September 2025. IPPR researcher Frederico Links said a section on data subject rights was removed from an earlier version in 2020 and, as far as he knew, had not been substantially restored. He also said the public had not seen the latest version. — [Namibian Sun, 18 Aug 2025](https://www.namibiansun.com/local-news/data-protection-bill-returns-to-parliament2025-08-18167195)
- 13 August 2025: The minister said the bill was in its final stages and expected in Parliament between September and October 2025. — [NAMPA, 13 Aug 2025](https://www.nampa.org/text/22709136)
- May 2025: The MICT's 2025/26 budget motivation (Vote 29) said the Data Protection Bill would be tabled in Parliament in the 2025/2026 financial year, once the Cabinet Committee on Legislation had dealt with it. That target has passed without a confirmed tabling. — [Parliament of Namibia, Vote 29 MICT 2025/26](https://www.parliament.na/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Vote-29-Information.pdf)
- Conflicting claims: Some aggregator sites say the bill was tabled in the National Assembly in September or October 2025, while adding that enactment is unconfirmed. — [anuragverma.co (aggregator)](https://anuragverma.co/worldwatch/namibia/data-privacy). This is contradicted by the minister's January 2026 statement that the bill was still due to return to the Cabinet Committee on Legislation ([New Era](https://neweralive.na/data-legislations-under-final-polishing/)). I treat the tabling claim as wrong.
- Conflicting claim: One policy document found by search refers to a "Data Protection Act (2022)" as governing personal data. — [NIPDB document](https://www.nipdb.com/_files/ugd/77942a_7d2a86e0d381435b9b2c828837cf6239.pdf). No other source supports this. DLA Piper (March 2026) and all Namibian press through January 2026 say no such Act exists. I treat the claim as an error.
- E-ID context: Civil society, including the ISOC Namibia Chapter, has criticised plans to roll out a chip-based e-ID (target July 2026) before a data protection law exists. — [Windhoek Observer](https://www.observer24.com.na/lack-of-data-protection-law-shadows-e-id-plan/)

**What the bill would impose (based on drafts; the current text is not public)**
- Regulator: a new, independent Data Protection Supervisory Authority. It could issue compliance assessments, investigate complaints, secure settlements, apply for warrants and publish information in the public interest. — [ENSafrica, 18 Nov 2024, on the 2023 Bill](https://www.ensafrica.com/news/detail/9421)
- Scope: the bill would cover automated and manual processing where the data is organised and accessible by specific criteria. It would apply to processing concerning individuals in Namibia and bind controllers, processors and third parties wherever they are located. It is not sector-specific. — [ENSafrica](https://www.ensafrica.com/news/detail/9421)
- Lawful processing: processing must be transparent, fair and lawful, and is "primarily" based on the data subject's consent. — [ENSafrica](https://www.ensafrica.com/news/detail/9421)
- Cross-border transfer: permitted where the recipient is subject to laws or agreements giving protection similar to Namibian standards, where the data subject consents, or where the transfer is necessary to perform a contract involving the data subject. — [ENSafrica](https://www.ensafrica.com/news/detail/9421)
- Special categories (race, political opinions, religious belief, trade union membership, criminal records): processing is prohibited unless the data subject consents or an exemption applies. Children's data is generally prohibited unless an exemption applies, and the controller must prove consent was obtained. — [ENSafrica](https://www.ensafrica.com/news/detail/9421)
- Penalties: fines, imprisonment or both. No amounts are published in the summaries I found. — [ENSafrica](https://www.ensafrica.com/news/detail/9421)
- Data subject rights: possibly weak or missing in the current draft. A data subject rights section was removed in 2020 and, per IPPR, not substantially restored. — [Namibian Sun](https://www.namibiansun.com/local-news/data-protection-bill-returns-to-parliament2025-08-18167195)

### Inferences
- MOTION's documents should say: "Namibia has not yet enacted a data protection law. A Data Protection Bill is pending (latest public status, January 2026: awaiting resubmission to the Cabinet Committee on Legislation). There is currently no regulator, registration duty or statutory breach-notification deadline." They should not cite a breach-notification hour count, a registration duty or a regulator as current Namibian law.
- The last published drafts follow the GDPR and POPIA pattern (independent authority, extraterritorial reach, consent-centred, adequacy-style transfer test). MOTION's off-site encrypted backups would most likely need an adequacy, consent or contract basis if they are held outside Namibia. Keeping backups in Namibia, or documenting the backup location and safeguards, is the low-risk design. This is an inference from draft text, not law.
- Because drafts make consent the main basis, MOTION's documents can sensibly describe processing as done for the workshop customer (controller) under contract, with MOTION as processor. That framing would carry over once the law arrives.

### Gaps
- No public text of the latest (2025/26) bill was found, so its breach-notification timeline, registration or notification duty, DPO requirement, processor duties and fine amounts are unknown. The 2022 draft PDF on dataprotection.africa returned HTTP 403.
- Whether anything happened between February and November 2026 (Cabinet Committee validation, tabling, first reading) is unconfirmed. Targeted searches of Namibian outlets found nothing dated after January 2026. One search-engine summary said a 2026/27 budget document reported the bill approved by the Cabinet Committee on Legislation and sent to the Ministry of Justice, but I could not find or verify that source.
- I could not read Cliffe Dekker Hofmeyr's alert of 7 October 2026 on Namibia's digital legal framework (HTTP 403), so I could not check what it says about the bill. — [CDH alert, 7 Oct 2026](https://www.cliffedekkerhofmeyr.com/news/publications/2026/Namibia/Corporate-Commercial/Corporate-and-Commercial-alert-7-October-2026-An-update-on-Namibias-digital-infrastructure-and-legal-framework)

## 2. What governs personal data now, and how it affects a SaaS provider

### Takeaway
Without a data protection act, the binding rules are four. First, the constitutional right to privacy (Article 13). Second, the Electronic Transactions Act 4 of 2019 (ETA): most of it has been in force since 16 March 2020, and section 20 (e-signatures) and Chapter 5 (voluntary security accreditation by CRAN) since 15 June 2026. Third, the Communications Act 8 of 2009, which binds telecoms licensees rather than SaaS providers. Fourth, sector rules and contract. ETA Chapter 4 (online consumer protection) is still not in force. Under the ETA, MOTION is a "service provider" because it provides data storage and backup. Its hosting safe harbour depends on publishing a designated agent for takedown notices.

### Cited Findings
**Constitution, Article 13**
- Article 13 protects privacy of homes, correspondence and communications. Interference is allowed only "save as in accordance with law and as is necessary in a democratic society", for listed interests such as national security, public safety, the country's economic well-being, health or morals, prevention of disorder or crime, and others' rights. — [DLA Piper: Namibia](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=NA); [UN Mission of Namibia, Constitution Chapter 3](https://www.un.int/namibia/namibia/chapter-3-fundamental-human-rights-and-freedoms). The phrase is quoted from a search summary of a Namibian legal commentary; check the exact wording against the [WIPO-hosted Constitution](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/222894).
- DLA Piper (March 2026) says sector-specific rules protect client information, particularly in legal and banking services. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=NA)

**Electronic Transactions Act 4 of 2019: commencement**
- The Act was signed by the President on 7 November 2019. It came into force on 16 March 2020 by GN 75/2020 (GG 7142), except section 20, Chapter 4 and Chapter 5. Section 20 and Chapter 5 came into force on 15 June 2026 by GN 182/2026 (GG 8949). — [LAC annotated statute, ETA 4 of 2019](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019.pdf)
- GN 182 of 2026 was signed by Minister Emma Theofelus on 19 May 2026 under section 60(2). It says "section 20 and Chapter 5 of that Act come into operation on the date of publication of this notice in the Gazette". It was published in Government Gazette No. 8949 of 15 June 2026. — [Government Gazette 8949, 15 June 2026](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/2026/8949.pdf)
- Chapter 4 (Consumer Protection, sections 34 to 40) is annotated "[This chapter is not yet in force.]". — [LAC annotated ETA](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019.pdf)
- Secondary sources conflict on dates: a UNECA profile says the Act came into force in March 2021 and lists e-signatures as not yet in force, and a law-firm summary presents the cooling-off right as effective from 2020. Both are contradicted by the gazetted notices above. — [UNECA country profile](https://www.uneca.org/sites/default/files/ATPC/cp/Digital%20trade%20regulatory%20integration%20-%20Country%20profile%20-%20Namibia-%20ENG.pdf); [itlawco](https://itlawco.com/focus-areas/it-law/electronic-transactions-act-4-of-2019-namibia/)

**ETA provisions in force that matter to MOTION**
- s19 and s20: a legal reference to "writing" includes a data message. A reference to a signature includes a "recognised electronic signature" (an advanced electronic signature meeting prescribed requirements). Under s20(2), an electronic signature that is not recognised can still be used "if parties agree to such use or if a law provides for such use". In force since 15 June 2026. — [LAC annotated ETA](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019.pdf)
- The Electronic Signature Regulations, 2025 (GN 335 of 2025) come into force on the date section 20 commences, so 15 June 2026. — [LAC, ETA Regulations 2025 GN 335](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoREG/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019-Regulations%202025-335.docx) (per search-result description; not read in full). A search summary of the CDH alert of 7 October 2026 says these regulations give a signature created outside Namibia the same legal effect if its reliability is substantially equivalent, and that between June and September 2026 Namibia launched a national digital trust infrastructure, DigiNam. — [CDH alert, 7 Oct 2026](https://www.cliffedekkerhofmeyr.com/news/publications/2026/Namibia/Corporate-Commercial/Corporate-and-Commercial-alert-7-October-2026-An-update-on-Namibias-digital-infrastructure-and-legal-framework) (not read directly; HTTP 403)
- s24, retention of electronic records: where a law requires records to be kept, keeping them electronically satisfies the law if four conditions are met. The record is a data message. It is kept in the format generated, sent or received, or one shown to represent it accurately. Its origin, destination and the dates and times of generation, sending, receipt and first retention can be identified. It meets any prescribed requirement. Under s24(3) a person may use another person's services to retain records, but "the former person will be liable" if that service fails to retain the information. — [LAC annotated ETA](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019.pdf)
- s25: computer evidence may not be held inadmissible solely because it is computer evidence. — [LAC annotated ETA](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019.pdf)
- Chapter 6 (in force since 2020): s49 defines a "service provider" to include anyone providing "any service relating to the hosting, or development of a website" or "any service relating to the storage or backup of data". Under s52, hosting providers are not liable for third-party data if they lack actual knowledge, are not aware of the infringing facts, and act expeditiously on a s54 take-down notice. Under s52(2), this protection applies only if the provider "has designated an agent to receive notifications" and publishes the agent's contact details on its service or website. Under s55, there is no general obligation to monitor. — [LAC annotated ETA](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019.pdf)
- Chapter 5 (in force 15 June 2026): CRAN ("the Authority", s1) may accredit security products or services, such as encryption, integrity, access control, tamper detection and signer linking. Suppliers "may apply", so accreditation is voluntary. — [LAC annotated ETA](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019.pdf)

**ETA Chapter 4: not in force, but written**
- s34 would require online suppliers to show contact details, a description, the full price including taxes, terms and how to keep a record of the transaction. The consumer must be able to review, correct and withdraw before ordering, and the supplier must use a "sufficiently secure" payment system and is liable for damage if it does not. s35 would give a 7-day cooling-off right. s36 deals with unsolicited communications and the use of the addressee's personal information. "Consumer" means a natural person. — [LAC annotated ETA](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019.pdf)

**Communications Act 8 of 2009 and CRAN**
- Part 6 of Chapter V (interception of telecommunications) came into force on 1 January 2023 by GN 292/2022 (GG 7917). Regulations under s77 were made in 2021 (GN 40/2021). — [LAC annotated Communications Act](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Communications%20Act%208%20of%202009.pdf) (per search-result extract); [GG 7481, 15 March 2021 (CRAN)](https://cran.na/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/GG-7481-dated-15-March-2021.pdf)
- IPPR (2024) argues the regulations create a legal basis for bulk communications surveillance. This is an advocacy view. — [IPPR, "No Privacy Guaranteed"](https://ippr.org.na/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/NO-PRIVACY-GUARANTEED-web-1.pdf)
- CRAN is the ETA "Authority" (s1). It publicly acknowledged in October 2025 that there is no comprehensive breach framework. — [LAC annotated ETA](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoSTAT/Electronic%20Transactions%20Act%204%20of%202019.pdf); [The Namibian, 3 Oct 2025](https://www.namibian.com.na/calls-for-urgent-finalisation-of-data-protection-law-after-nsfaf-data-breach/)

### Inferences
- For MOTION's security document, the accurate current-law statement covers six points. (a) Article 13 privacy is a constitutional right. (b) The ETA gives data messages, electronic records and, since 15 June 2026, electronic signatures legal effect. (c) Keeping records in MOTION satisfies legal retention duties only if the s24 integrity and metadata conditions are met; the workshop stays liable if MOTION fails (s24(3)). (d) MOTION is an ETA "service provider" and should publish a designated take-down agent to keep the s52 hosting protection. (e) There is no statutory breach-notification duty yet. (f) ETA consumer-protection duties (Chapter 4) are not yet in force.
- Signature capture: the "names on signatures" MOTION records (job-card sign-off, quote acceptance) are most likely ordinary electronic signatures. They are valid between parties who agree to them (s20(2)) but are not "recognised" (advanced, accredited) signatures. MOTION's terms should record that workshops and their customers agree to this method. Where a specific law requires a handwritten or recognised signature, MOTION's capture may not be enough.
- s24(1)(c) asks for origin, destination and first-generation and first-retention timestamps. MOTION's immutable document snapshots and audit log already match this. Saying so in the security document is accurate and useful.
- Article 13 is mainly enforceable against the state. Its direct effect between private parties (a SaaS vendor and a workshop's customers) is not established in the sources found. Contract, delict (unlawful invasion of privacy) and confidentiality are the practical private-law levers. This is an inference; I did not research Namibian case law.
- Communications Act interception and SIM duties bind telecoms licensees. A SaaS operator is not a licensee, though lawful-interception orders could reach its upstream carriers.

### Gaps
- I did not locate or read the text of GN 953 of 2025 under the ETA (listed by LAC), so its subject is unconfirmed. It may be accreditation regulations.
- No source confirmed any commencement date for ETA Chapter 4 after June 2026. The LAC annotation, current to at least GN 182/2026, still marks it not in force.
- I did not research employment-record rules (for example Labour Act 11 of 2007 record-keeping periods for mechanics' time and pay) or the Access to Information Act 2022's effect on records councils hold through a vendor. Both are relevant to MOTION's employee and council data but were outside the searches I ran.
- The exact Article 13 wording was not taken from an official primary text.

## 3. VAT in 2026: rate, thresholds, tax invoices and e-invoicing

### Takeaway
VAT is 15% and unchanged in 2026. Compulsory registration starts above N$1 million in taxable supplies in any 12 months (raised from N$500,000 by the VAT Amendment Act 5 of 2024), and voluntary registration is possible above N$200,000. Tax invoices are governed by s21 and Schedule VI of the VAT Act 10 of 2000. Cash sales up to N$1,000 need no tax invoice (raised from N$100 in 2024). Mandatory e-invoicing is planned but has no fixed date: the 2026/27 budget dropped the April 2026 target and moved to a phased rollout over 2026/27 to 2028/29.

### Cited Findings
- Standard rate 15%, with some supplies zero-rated and others exempt. Compulsory registration above NAD 1 million in any 12 months, voluntary above NAD 200,000 (with conditions). Page last reviewed 16 July 2026. — [PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries: Namibia, other taxes](https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/republic-of-namibia/corporate/other-taxes)
- The Value-Added Tax Amendment Act, 2024 (Act 5 of 2024), gazetted 16 September 2024, replaced the N$500,000 threshold with N$1,000,000. It also amended s21(2) so no tax invoice is needed for cash supplies up to N$1,000 (previously N$100). — [Government Gazette 8443, 16 Sept 2024](https://archive.gazettes.africa/archive/na/2024/na-government-gazette-dated-2024-09-16-no-8443.pdf) (per search-result extract); [PwC Namibia Tax First alert, Sept 2024](https://pwc.com/na/en/assets/pdf/tax-first-alert-september-2024-vat.pdf) (HTTP 403; quoted from search extract). The pre-amendment N$100 figure is in the [NamRA-hosted annotated VAT Act extract](https://www.namra.org.na/documents/cms/uploaded/schedule-vi-of-the-valueadded-tax-act-10-of-2000-23af2b8b8b.pdf).
- 2026/27 Budget (Finance Minister Ericah Shafudah, theme "People, Productivity, Prudence"; 9th MTEF 2026/27 to 2028/29): it proposes VAT amendments for agriculture input imports and the creative industry, and "Modernising the VAT Act for the implementation of e-invoicing". No rate change. — [KPMG Namibia 2026 Budget Summary, Feb 2026](https://assets.kpmg.com/content/dam/kpmgsites/za/pdf/2026/02/KPMG%20Namibia%202026%20Budget%20Summary.pdf.coredownload.pdf)
- E-invoicing timeline (15 June 2026): the earlier single April 2026 launch was dropped. The 2026/27 budget describes "a phased rollout spanning the next three years" within the 2026/27 to 2028/29 MTEF, aimed at "VAT-registered taxpayers". No phases, dates or technical model were published. — [KPMG TaxNewsFlash, 15 June 2026](https://kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2026/06/tnf-namibia-mandatory-e-invoicing-implementation-timeline-revised.html)
- A secondary briefing (April 2026) says planned e-invoices would carry all current tax-invoice fields plus a unique invoice ID. This is a proposal and unconfirmed by NamRA. — [VATupdate briefing, 27 Apr 2026](https://www.vatupdate.com/2026/04/27/briefing-document-podcast-e-invoicing-e-reporting-in-namibia/)
- s21 VAT Act: a registered supplier must, "on request", provide a tax invoice with the particulars in paragraph 1 of Schedule VI (s21(1)). Only one tax invoice may be issued per taxable supply (s21(4)). A registered recipient may request a missing invoice within 60 days of supply (s21(6)), and the supplier must comply within 14 days (s21(7)). A lost original may be replaced by a copy "clearly marked 'copy'" (s21(8)). Recipient-created tax invoices need the Commissioner's prior written approval and supplier agreement (s21(9)). s22 and Schedule VI paragraphs 2 and 3 cover tax credit and debit notes. Input tax deduction generally requires a s21 or s22 document. — [Annotated VAT Act 10 of 2000, ss18–22 (NamRA-hosted)](https://www.namra.org.na/documents/cms/uploaded/schedule-vi-of-the-valueadded-tax-act-10-of-2000-23af2b8b8b.pdf)
- Particulars (NamRA summary): the words "tax invoice"; the supplier's name, address and VAT number; the recipient's name and address; a description of goods; the total excluding VAT, the VAT charged and the total including VAT. "For a full list please see Schedule VI to the VAT Act." The same brochure says registered persons must keep accounting records in English "at the place of business in Namibia" and file returns by the 25th of the month after the period. The brochure dates from 2018 and still shows the old N$500,000 threshold. — [NamRA VAT brochure](https://itas.namra.org.na/assets/documents/other-forms/Value_Added_Tax_Brochure.pdf)

### Inferences
- MOTION's 15% VAT default is correct for 2026. No rate change is announced.
- MOTION's tax-invoice output should keep the s21 and Schedule VI basics: "Tax invoice" heading, supplier and recipient names and addresses, supplier VAT number, description, totals exclusive and inclusive of VAT, and VAT charged. It should enforce one original per supply, with reprints marked "copy" (s21(4), s21(8)), and support credit and debit notes (s22). MOTION's existing rule of taking tax from the document snapshot fits the one-invoice-per-supply rule.
- The NamRA brochure says records must be kept "at the place of business in Namibia". This deserves a line in MOTION's hosting document. A hosted system the workshop can access from its place of business, plus s24 ETA electronic retention, is the likely answer. However, Namibian hosting or a documented NamRA access arrangement lowers risk.
- E-invoicing will most likely arrive between 2027 and 2029 for VAT-registered businesses. MOTION should treat the invoice data model (unique IDs, structured fields) as a future integration point, not a current legal duty.

### Gaps
- I did not read Schedule VI paragraph 1 itself (the full list of particulars), so the exact field list, including any value threshold that changes the required recipient details, is unconfirmed.
- The commencement date of the N$1 million threshold (Act 5 of 2024) is unconfirmed. Sources say it was slated for FY2024/25, and PwC lists it as current in July 2026.
- The VAT record-retention period was not confirmed from a primary source.
- No NamRA technical specification for e-invoicing (clearance or reporting model, API, format) was found.

## 4. Public procurement: quotation and bidding thresholds for councils

### Takeaway
The thresholds were reset by GN 259 of 2026 (GG 8989), in force from 4 August 2026. It replaced Regulations 2 and 3 and Annexures 1 and 2 of the Public Procurement Regulations, 2017 under the Public Procurement Act 15 of 2015. For goods, works and non-consultancy services, method thresholds (VAT-inclusive) are: small value procurement up to N$30,000, request for sealed quotations up to N$3 million, and restricted bidding up to N$5 million. Open international bidding applies above N$45 million (goods) or N$70 million (works). All regional councils and Part I municipalities are now Category 1 entities: they run their own bidding up to N$45 million (goods), N$60 million (works), N$35 million (consultancy) and N$25 million (non-consultancy services). Above those limits the Central Procurement Board runs the bid.

### Cited Findings
- Amendment history: GN 47/2017 (from 1 April 2017), GN 129/2017, GN 78/2018, GN 297/2023 (15 September 2023), and GN 259/2026 (GG 8989), in force on publication on 4 August 2026. — [LAC annotated Public Procurement Regulations 2017](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoREG/Public%20Procurement%20Act%2015%20of%202015-Regulations%202017-047.pdf)
- GN 259/2026 was signed by Finance Minister Ericah Shafudah on 22 July 2026 and gazetted 4 August 2026. The Ministry said thresholds were adjusted for inflation and currency depreciation since 2017. Regional councils moved from Category 2 to Category 1 because decentralisation raised their procurement volumes. — [The Brief, 7 Aug 2026](https://thebrief.com.na/2026/08/namibia-streamlines-public-procurement-what-the-new-regulations-mean-for-government-and-business/); [The Brief, 7 Aug 2026 (second article)](https://thebrief.com.na/2026/08/public-entities-gain-powers-to-bypass-procurement-board-for-higher-value-tenders/)
- Regulation 2 (as substituted): the Central Procurement Board must run bidding for contracts that exceed an entity's Annexure 1 threshold. Within the threshold, the entity runs its own bidding. Entities are grouped as Category 1 to 3 plus high-value entities. Entities in Table 2 must submit an individual procurement plan to the Policy Unit at least 7 days before advertising any procurement above the Category 1 thresholds, and report on it within 7 days of award. — [LAC annotated Regulations](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoREG/Public%20Procurement%20Act%2015%20of%202015-Regulations%202017-047.pdf)
- Regulation 3 (as substituted): open international bidding (Act s30(a)) applies if the value exceeds the Annexure 2 threshold. Restricted bidding (s31(1)(b)), request for sealed quotations (s32(1)) and small value procurement (s38) apply if the value does not exceed it. Request for proposals (s35(2)) applies if the value is above it. All other methods are subject to Annexure 1 thresholds. Thresholds include VAT (reg 3(3)). — [LAC annotated Regulations](https://www.lac.org.na/laws/annoREG/Public%20Procurement%20Act%2015%20of%202015-Regulations%202017-047.pdf)
- Annexure 2 (from GN 259/2026), as read from the annotated statute's table layout:

  | Method | Goods | Works | Consultancy services | Non-consultancy services |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Open international bidding (s30(a)) | exceeds N$45m | exceeds N$70m | not applicable | not applicable |

### Inferences
- For MOTION's council purchase requisitions, vehicle parts are "goods" and outsourced repairs are most likely "non-consultancy services". The decision bands to encode (VAT-inclusive estimated value) would be:
  - up to N$30,000: small value procurement
  - up to N$3m: request for sealed quotations
  - up to N$5m: restricted bidding, where its statutory conditions are met
  - above that: open bidding by the council itself, up to the Category 1 limit (N$45m goods, N$25m non-consultancy services), and through the Central Procurement Board beyond it
- Thresholds should be configurable per tenant and per effective date. They changed on 4 August 2026 and regional councils changed category, so a requisition's value band should be judged against the rule in force on its date.
- The Act and Regulations set further conditions for each method, such as the number of quotations required and when restricted bidding is allowed. These were not read here and need checking before MOTION hard-codes quote counts.

### Gaps
- I did not read the minimum number of quotations required under s32 (sealed quotations) or s38 (small value procurement), or the related regulations, so MOTION's "number of quotes" rule is unconfirmed.
